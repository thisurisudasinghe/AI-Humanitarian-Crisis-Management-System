# Individual reflection — Member 4 (Operations)

My contribution was the operations slice: resources, shelters, volunteers and mapping, together with the database scripts, Postman collection, component diagram and report sections. I worked across most layers of the modular monolith, from the SQL scripts and domain entities to the ASP.NET Core controller, the application service and the Leaflet map components in our separate `/web` handoff app.

The decision I spent the most time on was where business rules should live. Earlier drafts had a controller that checked decisions and moved stock itself. I moved all of that behind `IOperationsService` so the controller only maps HTTP, roles and status codes. That made the transaction boundary explicit and testable: one transaction for the guarded stock or occupancy update, the row insert and the outbox notification. I also made the approved decision's `FinalPayloadJson` the sole authority for inventory, quantity, shelter and household size. It would have been easy to accept those values in the request body, but that would have let a caller bypass the recommender and the approver. The 403 decision gate and the conditional `UPDATE ... WHERE available >= @q` (and its shelter equivalent) are the two guards I am most confident about, because they fail closed even if the UI is wrong or two people click at the same time.

The hardest design problem was the request status transition after commit. I deliberately kept it outside the stock transaction so an unavailable case service cannot roll back stock that physically moved. That choice creates a recovery requirement rather than removing it. I added an audit entry with a retry hint and the `GET/POST /operations/status-transitions` pair, and I documented that replaying the same idempotency key is only a read of the existing allocation — the reliable recovery is the explicit retry endpoint. Writing that down forced me to be honest about the difference between "retry-safe" and "automatic".

Mapping was a different kind of challenge. I had to respect the frozen `IGeoService` contract while replacing the foundation's fail-closed stub with a real Nominatim/OSRM implementation. The adapters in `FrozenOperationAdapters.cs` let both interfaces resolve to the same service. I kept the cache TTLs asymmetric — 24 hours for success, at most a minute for failure — because caching a bad geocode for a day would be worse than making another call. The Haversine fallback is approximate and says so explicitly through `IsApproximate` and a null minutes value, so nothing downstream mistakes a straight line for a road route.

Testing changed my attitude to evidence. The build is green with zero warnings and the current suite passes 561/561, including fake-HTTP tests for the real geo cache and fallback. Those tests gave useful confidence, but they still use in-memory EF and fake handlers; they do not prove the 20-iteration LocalDB races or the real-timer 1 req/s throttle. I have listed those as planned rather than passed. Next time I would build the concurrency and timing harnesses first, then write the implementation against them, instead of treating them as end-of-project evidence. I would also verify the `/web` pages against the active `frontend/` shell earlier; keeping a separate handoff app helped us move quickly but left integration as a final team task.
